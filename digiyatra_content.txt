A reality-check on your notes, before we go further
The 29.1% figure is real — but it isn't a data leak. A January 2024 LocalCircles survey of roughly 21,000
Delhi-airport passengers found 29% had signed up for DigiYatra without realising it, and only 15% understood the
benefits and risks before signing up. It's a consent problem at the registration kiosk, not a breach of stored data —
and that distinction changes the fix (see M2 in Section 2).
The '30-day retention' note is real too, and it's not a rumour — it's in DigiYatra's own policy. Facial
biometrics are purged within 24 hours of flight departure, but non-biometric travel logs (your DigiYatra ID, PNR
hash, flight details) are retained for 30 days for audit purposes. The Internet Freedom Foundation has flagged
exactly this gap between the headline claim and the fine print (see M3).
Your '30 May 2026 — 10Cr — 38 airports' note matches the official record closely. The Ministry of Civil
Aviation announced on 30 May 2026 that DigiYatra had crossed 10 crore passenger journeys across 38 airports,
with 2.4 crore app downloads, and average entry processing cut from 15 seconds to 5. Good instinct to log it.
One thing has changed since you wrote those notes. DigiYatra Foundation is now in Delhi High Court against
its former technology vendor, Data Evolve Solutions, over who actually owns the platform and the passenger data
flowing through it. That turns 'data ownership' from a design choice into a live legal question — see Section 1.4.

1.3 Trust Boundaries
Four boundaries matter, and each new feature you add either respects them or quietly widens them:
• Device boundary — the phone-held wallet is meant to be the outermost trust root; nothing biometric is
supposed to leave it.
• Airport-local-server boundary — where a temporary token touches infrastructure DYF doesn't fully control
airport-by-airport.
• Vendor boundary — third-party FRT/backend contractors. This is exactly where the 2024 breach rumours took
hold: a legacy vendor was alleged to have an AWS-hosted endpoint receiving passenger data, contradicting the
“never leaves your phone” claim.
• Ecosystem-partner boundary — hotel/cab/lounge partners who can already receive contact details on an
opt-in basis today, and would receive more under your hotel-chain vertical.
Design rule for the two new verticals: extend the identity, don't extend the vault. A hotel or a baggage handler
should be able to verify a credential without ever holding the face embedding itself.
1.4 Data Ownership
This is no longer theoretical. On 29 October 2025, the Delhi High Court framed formal issues for trial in Digi
Yatra Foundation v. Data Evolve Solutions Pvt. Ltd. — a commercial dispute over who owns the Digi Yatra
Central Ecosystem platform itself, and the intellectual property in the services it renders. That single case will
determine practical control over the data pipeline that everything else in this document sits on top of.
The second half of the ownership problem is structural: because DYF is registered as a private Section-8
company, it falls outside the Right to Information Act, even though it runs infrastructure that is fast becoming
mandatory-adjacent for millions of travellers. Even a favourable court outcome wouldn't, by itself, give the public
a way to compel disclosure of day-to-day data handling.

Recommendation: don't inherit this ambiguity. Whatever entity operates your version of these features should

1.5 Authentication Mechanisms
Today: Aadhaar number plus OTP e-KYC via DigiLocker, a live selfie converted to an on-device face embedding
(reportedly a 512-dimension vector, AES-256 encrypted), and a temporary QR/token handed to the e-gate for a
live camera match. That's a single credential type for a single nationality — OCI holders and foreign passport
holders cannot enrol as of 2026, and there is no non-biometric second factor if a face match fails repeatedly,
beyond a full walk back to the manual lane.
Recommendation: tiered onboarding — Aadhaar e-KYC OR passport-and-visa OR a DigiLocker-issued
document, all normalised to the same on-device credential format, plus an app PIN or passkey as a fallback
factor so one failed face-match doesn't cost the entire time saving.
1.6 Consent Flows
Consent is nominally required before any biometric capture, but the January 2024 LocalCircles findings
described above, plus complaints raised in Parliament about kiosk staff pressuring passengers to sign up, show
the practice hasn't matched the policy. The Foundation's main fix so far has been deploying human “Digi
Buddies” to advise passengers at the kiosk — a people-process patch on what is fundamentally an interface
problem.
Recommendation: move primary consent into the app itself rather than a kiosk conversation — a layered,
plain-language “what, why, how long, who else” screen before any capture, a persistent toggle with one-tap
revocation, and a consent receipt saved into the user's own wallet so they hold their own proof of what they
agreed to.
1.7 Interoperability Requirements
• Aadhaar / DigiLocker — already integrated for onboarding.
• Airline DCS/PSS systems — integrated today, but per-airline, with no shared standard.
• Multiple e-gate hardware vendors within India (including NEC) — no confirmed shared token format across
vendors yet.
• IATA One ID — the real global benchmark, built on W3C Verifiable Credentials and ICAO's Digital Travel
Credential standard (Doc 9303). In April 2026, Japan Airlines passengers transferred from Tokyo through Hong
Kong to London using a single digital identity recognised by Hong Kong International, Heathrow, and British
Airways — that is the bar the Foundation's own 2025 Changi and Dubai MoUs need to clear.
• RailOne — Indian Railways' own biometric pilot; a natural domestic interoperability target so one credential
works at both airports and stations.
• New: hotel PMS systems and the Bureau of Immigration's e-FRRO / Form C portal.
• New: airport cargo/customs systems and RFID/IoT baggage backbones.
Recommendation: adopt the IATA One ID token format from the start for anything you build, even before
international expansion is live. Retrofitting a proprietary token later is exactly the kind of rework the Foundation
now faces with its own Changi/Dubai pilots.
1.8 Governance Touchpoints
• MoCA sets policy; AAI is both a 26% DYF shareholder and the country's largest airport operator/regulator —
worth designing around as a structural conflict of interest, not just an org-chart line.
• BCAS issues the operating security circulars.
• The DPDP Act 2023 is the general law, but the Data Protection Board meant to enforce it is, per a pending
Kerala High Court PIL, still not fully operational.

• Delhi HC (ownership) and Kerala HC (biometric-privacy PIL) are, in effect, doing the job a dedicated statute

• DYF's Section-8 status keeps it outside the RTI Act.
Recommendation: don't wait for the state to close these gaps top-down. Build the transparency the law doesn't

1.9 Operational Challenges
• Tier-3/smaller-airport hardware and connectivity lag — the Foundation's own 2026 rollout batches are still
activating roughly 60 smaller stations.
• Peak-hour camera throughput, plus accuracy variance across lighting, masks and demographics.
• Staff training at scale as new airports and features roll out.
• Inconsistent public messaging about what's voluntary versus mandatory — as recently as June 2026, credible
outlets disagreed on whether international-transit use had become compulsory at four metro airports. That
confusion is itself an operational failure, not just a communications one.
• Several other digital initiatives (self-baggage-drop, ATC automation, the AirSewa grievance portal, AI-driven
“digital twins”) are rolling out in parallel, raising real integration and support load.
1.10 Expected Public Benefits
• Verified time savings at national scale: average entry processing cut from about 15 seconds to about 5, across
100 million-plus journeys.
• Less paper and physical document handling.
• Better capacity planning as India heads toward its own projected 50-crore-passenger year by 2030.
• A reusable trust rail that, if the two new verticals are built carefully, extends the same public benefit to
hospitality compliance and airport logistics — not just the security queue.

New Verticals: Hotel Chains & Supply Chain
Both of these extend the same verified-identity rail outward from the airport gate — they should feel like the
same app, not a bolt-on.
Figure 2. Proposed DigiYatra 2.0 ecosystem — core spine plus the two new verticals and the governance fixes from Section 1.
3.1 Hotel Chain Integration
This is a smaller leap than it looks. Every hotel in India already has a legal duty to collect and file near-identical
data: a Guest Registration Card for every guest, and for foreign nationals specifically, a Form C filed to the
Bureau of Immigration within 24 hours of check-in and again at checkout, under the Foreigners Act 1946 and the
Registration of Foreigners Rules 1992. DigiYatra's ecosystem-partner framework already lets passengers opt in
to share contact details for hotel bookings today — this is the natural next step, not a foreign add-on.
• Pre-arrival digital check-in — share the verified identity and stay dates with the hotel before arrival,
auto-populating the Guest Registration Card and Form C so front-desk staff stop retyping passport details by
hand.
• Biometric room access — reuse the same face token for room entry instead of a separate hotel app or key
card.
• Automated checkout — auto-file the checkout Form C the moment a guest checks out, closing a compliance
step hotels currently do manually.
• Family / group stays — reuse the Group-PNR linking from Section 2 (m4) so a family booked under one flight
PNR checks into one hotel room without re-entering everyone's details.
• Optional loyalty linking — strictly opt-in, and kept separate from the identity vault itself.
Guardrail: nothing here should collect data hotels aren't already legally required to collect — it just gets
collected once instead of twice. Keep a manual front-desk fallback, exactly like the airport's manual lane, and
start with citizen/Aadhaar-based guests before extending to the multi-credential onboarding proposed in M6,

3.2 Supply Chain & Airport Logistics
Airport logistics research typically treats cargo, baggage and catering handling as one connected problem, and
RFID/IoT baggage tracking synced to a passenger's own app is an established pattern elsewhere in the industry.
DigiYatra already creates a PNR-hash-linked journey record for every trip — that record is a natural anchor for a
bag tag, without needing a new identity system.
• RFID/BLE bag tracking — link a bag tag to the passenger's verified journey record; show real
loaded/unloaded status in the Utility tab, aimed at cutting mishandling, not just showing a map pin.
• Ground-crew & vendor identity badges — replace manual paper gate-passes for catering, fuel and cargo
staff with the same credential-verification core used for passengers.
• Cargo pre-clearance data bridge — mirror IATA's own passenger pre-clearance logic for goods, sharing
customs data with airline cargo systems ahead of arrival.
Guardrail, and it's an important one: goods and vendor identities are a different trust boundary from traveller
biometrics (Section 1.3). Build this as its own module with its own access-control list behind the Utility tab —
never let a baggage-tracking bug become a path into the passenger identity vault.
04
App Architecture — Making Two Tabs Work Hard
Keeping this to two tabs is good information-architecture discipline — most identity “super-apps” fail by
fragmenting into six or more tabs that no one fully explores. The fix for everything in Sections 2 and 3 is to load
these two tabs well, not to add a third.
Tab What lives there
Authentication Identity creation & verification (multi-credential, M6) · document wallet (DigiLocker-linked) · the single
consent/retention screen (M2, M3) · Group-PNR / family management (m4) · a Trust Center (audit log,
breach history, one-tap revoke — M4).
Utility Flight & gate tracking (m3) · SOS (m7) · web check-in · baggage tracking (3.2) · hotel check-in module
(3.1) · offline token cache (m5) · wheelchair / accessibility booking.
On the ideas you X'd out (conveyor-belt details, airport taxi integration, airport maps, sleeping-pod booking,
wheelchair booking): that's your call to make and there may well have been good reasons to cut them for scope.
Worth knowing only that airport maps and wheelchair/assistance booking are the cheapest of the five to bring
back later, since they can sit as thin, low-maintenance entries inside the Utility tab rather than needing dedicated
back-end work — useful if scope opens up again, not a reason to revisit the call now.
05
Public Policy, Cost, and Speed
5.1 Policy Alignment
• Design for DPDP Act 2023 compliance from day one — purpose limitation, the right to erasure, and data
portability — rather than retrofitting it.
• Internet Freedom Foundation's core asks (statutory backing, RTI-equivalent transparency) are a useful
checklist your own project can model even before government policy catches up.
• Treat the still-unresolved Data Protection Board timeline as a reason to over-invest in your own Trust Center
(M4), not a reason to wait for regulation to force your hand.

5.2 Cost Strategy
• Reuse India Stack rails (Aadhaar, DigiLocker, UPI) instead of building parallel KYC or payment infrastructure.
• Prefer open-source liveness/face-match models and on-device inference to cut both licensing and server
bandwidth costs.
• Phase hardware rollout to high-traffic airports first, mirroring the Foundation's own batch approach instead of
duplicating the sequencing work.
• A visible Trust Center (M4) reduces distrust-driven support tickets — transparency is a cost saver here, not just
an ethics line item.
5.3 Speed & Performance
• The verified national benchmark to beat is 5 seconds average entry processing — not just faster than a manual
queue, faster than DigiYatra's own current number.
• Cache credentials before arrival, use a CDN for app assets, and keep the APK lightweight for lower-spec
Tier-2/3 devices.
• For the international piece, the standard to match is the April 2026 IATA proof-of-concept where Japan Airlines
passengers transferred Tokyo–Hong Kong–London on one digital identity recognised by three different
operators — and Dubai Airport's long-standing benchmark of cutting passenger time by up to 40% with
comparable technology.
